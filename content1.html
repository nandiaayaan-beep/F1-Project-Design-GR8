About the Sport

The sport Formula 1 is played from the months of March till December. The sport is a high speed intense motorsport featuring 11 teams and 22 drivers. Each Driver and Team aim to win championships which can be achieved by gaining points through race wins. If a Driver wins the most points, they gain a world championship, and if a team does, the team gains a constructor's championship. A driver can win a world championship, but a team itself cannot, as a team consists of the points for both the drivers. The following point format is followed: 25-18-15-12-10-8-6-4-2-1 for 1st-2nd-3rd-4th-5th-6th-7th-8th-9th-10th. Teams are able to modify their car at any given time, as long as it stays within the rules of the FIA regulations. And so, it is a battle of both skill, and engineering.


Formula 1 cars can reach speeds of over 300kmph, with the highest recorded speed being 372.6 kmph.

The key defining factors of F1 cars are infact their aerodynamics, not their engines, with multiple other cars featuring horsepower's significantly higher than F1 cars, with better V8 and V10 turbocahrged engines.

Formula 1 drivers can face G-forces of up to 5 Gs during high speed corners. Drivers of this sport have to train their neck muscles to withstand these superhuman forces!

The first Formula 1 World Championship was held in 1950, and the sport has since evolved into a global phenomenon with millions of fans worldwide.


What makes an F1 car so fast

The Engine

To begin, we must first cover the key components of a car which make it fast. The most important thing is: The engine, being the greatest defining factor of the speed of a car. F1 cars feature a turbocharged 1.6 litre V6 hybrid engine that features about 560 horespower in the MGU-H, with the MGU-K motor adding 560 horsepower to the engine following breaking, or actions which allow for the recovery of energy lost from breaking. Most regular cars feature only V6 Hybrids.

Gearboxes

F1 cars also feature a semi automatic 8 gear gearbox, which uses a button to change the gears. A car has only 5 gears and a handle which has to be pulled back holding the role of changing the gears. As a result, the power conservation on regular cars is extremely poor, and has bad energy optimisation due to the lack of options. The slow nature of shifting the gearbox rod results in slower gear changes. Additionally, a regular car's gearbox also takes 0.05 seconds to change gears, while F1 cars take 0.02 seconds, being 250% faster than the speed of gear switching in not just regualular cars, but in some cases - supercars and sportscars which lack more advanced gear systems.

Aerodynamics

Aerodynamics aren't the largest factor for speed, but make the greatest difference when it comes to separating F1 cars from regular cars. You see, although the engine is the most defining factor, many cars share V6 if not higher V8 and V10 engines, with many supercars having turbocharged engines which are significantly better than that of an F1 car. An F1 car excels in aerodynamics, being built around purely creating downforce. Downforce is a force which helps in pushing cars down. This phenomena creates stronger grip in the cars, which allows for them to perform extremely fast turns at high speeds, alongside getting faster speeds on straights from better tire grip. An F1 car generates a whopping 2000-3500kgs of downforce. The front wing contributes ot ~25% of the downforce, floor and ground effect tunnels accumulate for about ~50%, the rear wing giving upto ~15% of the downforce, with the diffuser being responsible for the remaining ~10% of the downforce. As a result, an F1 car can significantly outperform other cars in consistent speed through straights and corners. An F1 car doesn't excel in straights, but it's strong point remains in corners


Supercars, Sportscars, and Hypercars; Why they all pale in comparison to F1 cars

When discussing supercars, we have to divide them into 3 different categories: sportscars, supercars, and hypercars.

To begin, sportscars are what you buy when you want to balance speed with comfort. Sports cars can commonly also be 4 seaters unlike their supercar and hypercar counterparts, and often feature V6 and V8 engines, with very few of them actually being hypercharged. They also lack aerodynamic features such as a rear wing. Supercars take this a step further, moving into much stronger engines, potentially being turbocharged. Supercars and sports cars have a very blurry line, with many defining factors including a more exotic design for a supercar, 2 seaters instead of 4, and high performance mildly large engines. Hypercars are where the focus on the features of car shifts from aesthetics and performance, to pure performance. Supercars can also feature aerodynamic elements, such as a rear wing, front splitter, diffuser etc. although not even close to the level of these components in F1. Hypercars are monsters which are purely focused on performance. They feature extremely powerful turbocharged v8, and v10 engines, having horsepowers up to 3000 horsepower (being the fastest modern day car in a straight), and top speeds of up to 496kmph as seen in the fastest invented hypercar. They often take direct inspiration from F1 aerodynamics, implementing much better defined and larger rear wings and front splitters, in some cases even entire front wings, alongside advanced and massive diffusers, aero-focused body shapes, cooling, air channels which go through the car, and much more. So, the question arises, if these cars feature horsepowers so high, why aren't they just used in F1?

The answer is simple: F1 cars are purely focused on performance, and are not designed for comfort. They are designed to be as light as possible, and have a very low drag coefficient. You could argue: Multiple hypercars feature engines several times stronger than in an F1 car, and you would be right. On a straight, they would be faster than an F1 car. But, F1 cars are built to be light, and create extreme downforce. This downforce gives them trmemendous grip on turns, allowing them to take corners at much higher speeds than hypercars can. A hypercar can corner at 1.5G, while an F1 car can corner at 5G.

Timeline

1950s

F1 began in the 1950s when racing gained popularity during 1901. Some of the first supercars included Alberto Ascari, Stirling Moss, and Juan Manuel Fangio. The first major change in F1 regulations was in 1958 which included homologation of fuel, track size limits, and arrival of rear-engined cars. During this time, F1 was composed of 100,000 - 200,000 spectators who watched live matches. This is merely a fraction of F1's current fanbase.

1962

A new team named Lotus led by coilin chapman came into place, which would develop some of the most important cars in F1 history. In 1962, Lotus used an aluminium sheet monocoque chassis to replace heavier steel frames that cas were built around. This monocoque was lighter, stronger, and much more stable and unbeatable in the hands of Jim Clark. The Ford-Cosworth DFV offered teams a superb engine that was powerful, reliable, and easy to maintain. In 1970, the true era of aerodynamics began. In the late 60s, wings began appearing on cars, but they had become increasingly ungainly and unsafe.

1970s

In the 1970s, the FIA standardised the rules and F1 entered the age of aerodynamics. Wings began appearing on cars, but they had become increasingly ungainly and unsafe. In the 1970s, the FIA standardised the rules and F1 entered the age of aerodynamics. Things such as sidepods, over the rear wing and side-mounted were invented, with airflow under the car being created from developed front wings to increase overall grip and speed. Furthermore, the 1970s was a huge milestone for the development of F1, bringing a commercial side to it, streaming rights, and establishment of FOCA. Ecclestone became a central figure in the F1 circle and reshaped the business landscape of the sport itself. He transformed the sport into what it is today, making it much more large and powerful. By the 1970s, Teams were operating themselves. He established the FOCA to ensure the teams were united with each other, and also featured clear communication. It also ensured commercial brands would receive more recognition in the sport.

1990s

The 1990s saw a turbocharged breakthrough in engines, with the first ever turbocharged car being released in 1977. The engines were powerful but unreliable. They were quite slow to take off, but powerful long term. In the 1990s to 2000s, teams piled sophisticated tech into their cars, such as semi-automatic gearboxes with traction control. In 1994, a bid to slow down cars and put driver skill forward, made the FIA ban electronic driver aids such as traction control, launch control, and active suspension. This made cars much harder to control and led to Senna’s fatal crash at Imola. This tragedy resulted in safety measures, better helmets, pit lane speed limits, and a wooden- skid block to be implemented.

2000s

Red bull racing rose in 2009, with the F1 introducing a new set of rules bringing in KERS, in response to climate and economic cost. With engine output standardised, red bull racing thrived with brilliant chassis designs. The lat 2 major F1 rule changes had a major effect on the Red Bull racing team and their approach to 2026.

2014-Modern day

In 2014, the emphasis switched from chassis design to engine power. Turbochargers were back, this time as a component of new, smaller,and fuel efficient hybrid recovery. This made F1 more sustainable and relevant to cat technology. In the modern era, the cars are going to be the most fuel efficient cars in the series. The current hybrid power units are extremely efficient. From 2026, the hybrid unit will produce three times more power.
